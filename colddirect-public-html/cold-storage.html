How commercial cold storage fails in London

London stores live in basements with poor airflow, on roofs with pigeon mesh, and in yards where the condenser sits behind bins. A dirty condenser in a heatwave is the July pattern. An iced evaporator after a door left open during a night delivery is the winter pattern. A failed door heater is the handle-wrenching pattern. Shared packs that serve a chill store and a freeze store on one rack are the “both rooms feel wrong” pattern. Vibration from street traffic loosens flares on split pipework. Power cuts reset controllers to factory set points that are useless for high-risk food.

Holding temperature is the point of cold storage. A chill store should sit near 1 to 4 °C. A freeze store should sit near minus 18 to minus 21 °C. If the display says 2 °C and a packed tray in the middle of a pallet says 9 °C, you already have an airflow or loading problem, not just a “gas” problem. We probe product, not only the air at the door. That is how we stop a false all-clear.

What we do on the first visit

We walk the store, the door, the evaporator and the outdoor unit. We check fans, defrost, drain, heaters and controller history if the display stores it. We look at loading: pallets hard against the evaporator, boxes on the floor blocking the return, strip curtains tied back. Those site habits cause as many warm stores as failed compressors. If the charge looks light we leak-test. If the compressor is the fault we quote the swap against the age of the pack so you can decide repair versus replacement before we start a long job.

Access matters. Have the roof hatch key, the plant-cage padlock and a member of staff who knows which isolator belongs to which room. A cold storage call-out that waits twenty minutes for a missing key is a wasted emergency slot.

Chill store versus freeze store

A chill store and a freeze store share panels and a door. They do not share defrost strategy, oil management or heater kits. Mixing those jobs on a phone call is how the wrong parts arrive. Tell us whether the room is meant to be plus 3 or minus 18. If you run both, say so. Shared packs need a pipework map, not a guess.

London plant-room realities

  • Basement stores with a condenser in a cupboard next to a boiler: no make-up air, short cycling, oil logged in the condenser.
  • Roof packs with blocked mesh: high pressure trips that staff reset until the compressor dies.
  • Strip curtains tied back “for speed”: moist air, iced coils, water on the floor, then a call at 06:00.
