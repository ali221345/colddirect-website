Cold Room Temperature Fluctuating? 4 Checks to Run

Updated 9 Oct 2026 · 8 min read

Mobile 07983 759320 Freephone 0800 112 3427 London Office 0203 952 4822

A walk-in that yo-yos between “fine” and “worrying” is more dangerous than a room that has simply died. When the readout jumps three or four degrees every hour, staff assume the plant is recovering. High-risk food in the middle of the pallet may already be in the danger zone while the air sensor sits in a cold draught near the evaporator.

Use this as a manager’s checklist before you phone for a van. It covers chilled rooms in restaurants, butchers and convenience stores. It will not replace a gauge set or a leak search. It will stop you calling an engineer for a door that has been tied open with twine since lunch.

Why Fluctuation is Dangerous

UK food businesses are expected to keep chilled food under control, not merely “quite cold”. A room that spends part of the day at 3 °C and part at 11 °C creates a false log. The paper record may show a lucky snapshot. The chicken on the third shelf does not care about snapshots.

Fluctuation also wrecks plant. Short-cycling a compressor, or running it against an iced coil, shortens the motor’s life. Condensation on walls becomes mould. Floor ice becomes a slip. In a small London kitchen the cold room is often the only legal storage for the whole menu. Losing it mid-service is not an inconvenience; it is a close-down.

Separate two problems in your head. Air temperature on the controller is not the same as food core temperature. A cheap infrared gun on a cardboard box is not a core reading. Use a cleaned probe in the food if you are deciding whether to discard stock.

Night set-backs and “eco” modes on some controllers can look like a fault. If someone changed a parameter last week, write down the current set point and differential before you blame the condenser.

Four checks for a cold room temperature swing

1. Thermostat and probe

Look at the controller. Is the set point still what you think it is? A finger-poke on a wet panel can drop a room from 2 °C to 8 °C without anyone admitting it. Compare the display with a second thermometer hung in the same air stream, away from the door and away from the coil.

Follow the probe cable if you can see it. A probe that has fallen onto the evaporator will read extra-cold and starve the room. A probe hanging in the door opening will read extra-warm and run the plant to death. Clip it back to the manufacturer’s position, usually in the return air.

If the display is blank, flickering, or showing a sensor error code, stop pressing random buttons. Photograph the code and the data plate. That is an electrical or controller job.

2. Door curtain and seal

Strip curtains only work if the blades hang straight and overlap. Missing blades, or blades rolled up “just for service”, turn the room into an air-conditioned corridor. Check the gasket on the door leaf the same way you would on a cabinet: even contact, no tears, no missing magnet.

Watch one busy hour. If the door is open more than it is shut, no evaporator in a restaurant yard will hold a flat line. Fit a closer if the leaf swings free. Train the pass so only one person fetches, not a procession.

3. Condenser blocked

The condenser is usually on the roof of the room or on a remote pack in a yard or cellar. Grease from extract, plus London dust, mats the fins. The plant then rejects heat poorly. Head pressure rises. The room hunts: it cools slowly, then overshoots, then cuts out.

If the pack is accessible and isolated, a soft brush and a vacuum on the inlet face can restore a surprising amount of capacity. Do not pressure-wash electrics. If the pack is on a flat roof you cannot reach safely, do not climb. That is why you book a visit.

4. Ice on the evaporator

Open the evaporator guard only if you are allowed to and the isolator is off. A coil packed with ice cannot absorb heat. Air temperature then drifts up until a defrost, then crashes, then ices again. That sawtooth is a classic “fluctuation” complaint.

If you see ice, do not attack it with a screwdriver. A controlled defrost with stock moved is the safe path. If ice is back the next day, the defrost heater, drain or door load needs a trade diagnosis. Book cold room repair in North London rather than repeating overnight melt-downs.

Common Technical Faults

After the four checks, remaining faults are usually: failed evaporator fan (warm top shelves, cold floor), failed condenser fan, leaking liquid-line valve, low refrigerant from a leak, or a controller that has lost its programme after a power cut. London basements add a special case: a pack fighting 35 °C plant-room air in summer because the intake is in a cupboard.

Expansion valves and electronic controllers need gauges and superheat readings. If your “fix” is turning the thermostat to its coldest stop, you are masking a dirty condenser or a starved coil and you will freeze the produce on the top rail while the door end stays warm.

Keep a simple log: time, displayed air temp, door-open events, and whether a delivery just landed. Three days of notes often show the fault is behavioural, not mechanical. Engineers prefer that log to a vague “it’s been funny all week”.

Cost of Ignoring

Ignoring a swinging room costs more than a call-out. You pay in discarded protein, emergency wholesale runs, overtime, and in the worst case an EHO improvement notice. A compressor that has been short-cycling for a month is a four-figure plant bill, not an eighty-pound visit.

Insurance claims for “gradual spoilage” are often declined if you cannot show you acted when the log first went out of range. Moving stock the same afternoon is cheaper than arguing with a loss adjuster.

If the room is already warm and you are losing product tonight, treat it as an emergency rather than a Monday job. Use our cold room repair in London team or freezer room repair London for walk-in frozen stores, and call to discuss whether to hold stock in another cabinet or to scrap it. Do not leave high-risk food in a room that cannot prove its temperature.

Once the plant is stable again, put the room on a simple service interval: condenser clean, door furniture, probe check, and a look at ice on the coil. Fluctuation that returns every summer is usually dirt and door traffic, not a mystery refrigerant disease.
