How a walk-in fridge is built

Most London walk-in fridges are 80 mm or 100 mm PIR panels with a rebated door, a heated frame, a PVC strip curtain that nobody puts back, and a ceiling-mounted evaporator with electric defrost. The condensing unit might be a Copeland, Bitzer, Maneurop or a packaged Foster / Adande / Viessmann pack. Split pipework runs through a ceiling void, a riser or a backyard. Vibration from traffic, tube lines and roof plant loosens joints. That is a London-specific leak pattern we see more than a “random” failed compressor.

Some sites run two evaporators on one room. If one fan bank seizes, the other cannot carry a Friday delivery. Some sites share a pack with a freezer room. A fridge fault on a shared pack can look like a freezer problem until we map the solenoids. We will not guess that from the street. We walk the plant, then we quote.

Faults unique to walk-in fridges

Ice on the evaporator after a door left open during a meat delivery is the weekday classic. The coil becomes insulation. Air stops moving. Product on the top rail still looks cold while the floor cases warm. A failed door heater lets the gasket freeze to the frame so staff wrench the handle and tear the seal. A stuck defrost clock ices the coil solid even when the door discipline is good. A dirty roof condenser in summer cannot reject heat; the room hunts and the compressor trips on high pressure. Drain trays freeze and dump water onto stock. Controllers lose their set point after a power cut and run the room at 10 °C all night while nobody looks at the display.

Low refrigerant after a leak is quieter. The compressor still runs. The room just never quite gets back after a delivery. We weigh the charge and find the joint. We will not top up and drive away. A recharge without a repair is how the same walk-in fridge fails again the next week.

What to do before the engineer arrives

Keep the door shut. Put the strip curtain back. Stop adding warm deliveries into a climbing room. Move high-risk items to a working cabinet if you have one. Photograph the controller, the data plate on the condensing unit, and the evaporator. Clear access to the roof hatch or the plant cage. Do not chip ice off an evaporator with a knife. Do not reset a high-pressure switch ten times. Those two habits turn a first-visit repair into a compressor replacement.

Why London walk-in fridges ice up

  • Deliveries prop the door for twenty minutes; moist air loads the coil and the next defrost cannot clear it.
  • Roof condensers sit under pigeon mesh that nobody cleans; head pressure rises in a July heatwave and the room will not pull down after lunch.
  • A failed frame heater lets the gasket freeze; staff force the door and the seal splits along the hinge.

Shared packs and split systems

Some kitchens run the walk-in fridge and a small freezer on one pack. A fridge solenoid that sticks open will pull the freezer down and flood the fridge evaporator. That is not a “gas leak” story. It is a control story. We map the pipework before we recover refrigerant. Guessing on a shared pack is how you lose both rooms.
